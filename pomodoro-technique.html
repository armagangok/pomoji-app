The Pomodoro Technique, explained

A simple method for getting started, staying focused and taking breaks that actually restore you.

By the Pomoji team · Updated September 24, 2026

The Pomodoro Technique is a way of working in short, timed bursts of full attention with small breaks in between. It was developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, who timed his study sessions with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. Pomodoro is Italian for tomato, and the name stuck.

It is popular because it is almost too simple to fail: you do not need a system, a notebook or a new app to start. You need one task, one timer and the willingness to ignore everything else until it rings.

How the Pomodoro Technique works

  1. Pick one task. Not a to-do list, one thing: a chapter, a bug, a draft section.
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task until it rings.
  3. Take a 5-minute break. Stand up, drink water, look away from screens.
  4. Repeat. After four focus sessions, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

One session, one target. That is the whole trick: the timer turns a vague intention into a small, winnable goal.

Why 25 minutes?

Twenty-five minutes is long enough to get real work done and short enough that starting feels easy. It lowers the cost of beginning, which is where most procrastination lives. The break is not a reward you have to earn; it is part of the method. Regular pauses help you notice when you are drifting and keep the next session fresh.

That said, 25 minutes is a starting point, not a rule. Many people settle on something that fits their work better:

What to do during breaks

Breaks work best when they are a real change of state. Good breaks move your body or rest your eyes: stretch, walk to the window, refill your water, breathe. Breaks that pull you into another screen, such as social media or messages, tend to steal the attention you were trying to recover and make the next session harder to start.

Pomo's favorite short break is a big stretch. Your shoulders will thank you too.

Handling interruptions

Cirillo's rule is simple: a session is indivisible. If something pops into your head, write it down and get back to the task. If a real interruption arrives, either deal with it later or end the session and start a fresh one. Over time you will notice patterns in what interrupts you, which is useful information in itself.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping breaks when you feel good. Momentum is great, but skipping every break usually costs you later in the day.
  • Choosing tasks that are too big. "Write thesis" is not a session. "Draft the introduction's first paragraph" is.
  • Letting the phone decide. Put it face down, or let your timer own the Lock Screen so checking the time does not turn into checking everything.
  • Treating the count as a score. Four honest sessions beat eight distracted ones.

Frequently asked questions

Who invented the Pomodoro Technique?

Francesco Cirillo developed it in the late 1980s while studying at university, timing his work with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer.

How long is one Pomodoro?

Traditionally 25 minutes of focused work followed by a 5-minute break. After four sessions you take a longer break of about 15 to 30 minutes.

Can I change the length of a Pomodoro?

Yes. Many people use 50/10 for deep work or 15/3 on low-energy days. The key idea is a fixed focus block followed by a real break.
